Research focus

Evaluation instruments for personalization

Personalization is where AI most often acts for someone rather than answering them. That makes it the right venue for a measurement problem: how do you verify a system deserves the trust already placed in it?

Most evaluation in this space measures a model against a held-out slice of the same distribution that trained it. That answers a narrow question well and a useful one badly. The failures that matter in deployment are the ones a clean test split cannot see: an identity that leaks between splits and inflates accuracy, a population that drifts over years while the benchmark stays fixed, an explanation that reads fluently and does not describe what the model actually did.

So my work builds instruments rather than models: longitudinal datasets that make drift measurable, evaluation protocols that separate leakage from genuine generalisation, and audits that test whether a system's account of itself is faithful.

Longer term I am working toward the same measurement problem for agentic systems, where a model acts on someone's behalf rather than only advising them, and the cost of an unverified decision stops being a bad suggestion. The work below does not include agents yet. It builds the benchmarks and audits that evaluating an agent would need first.

All of it sits on the production work further down. Nearly eight years of shipping recommenders, vision pipelines and multi-tenant inference is where the failure modes came from: silent degradation, confident wrong output, metrics that looked fine until a real population met them. The research is an attempt to measure what I kept having to patch.

Separating what a benchmark measures from what it merely remembers

Visual phenotyping models for livestock report strong numbers. Six years of the same marketplace gave us a way to ask what those numbers are made of. BoviShift is a multi-attribute audit built on 2,657 unique bulls photographed between 2021 and 2026, long enough that the population itself moves underneath the task.

The core contribution is a three-way protocol decomposition. A conventional random split conflates two very different things: a model recognising an animal it has already seen under a new photograph, and a model generalising to animals it has not. Splitting the evaluation into successive protocols isolates identity leakage from temporal distribution shift, so the accuracy drop can be attributed rather than reported as a single degraded figure. The same decomposition applies anywhere a benchmark is assembled from repeat observations of the same entities, which describes most personalization data.

Excluded by design

The 2024 cohort is held out of the primary temporal arm. Its missingness is non-random, and including it would have let a collection artifact masquerade as distribution shift. That is the error the protocol exists to prevent.

When a recommender explains itself, is the explanation true?

Language models have made recommendation explanations fluent. Fluency is not faithfulness. An LLM asked why it surfaced an item will produce a reason whether or not that reason had anything to do with the ranking, and users reasonably treat a stated reason as a real one. That gap is measurable, and largely unmeasured.

This audit tests three properties together: whether explanations describe the actual basis of a recommendation, whether the system fabricates attributes of items it recommends, and whether either failure lands unevenly across groups. Running the whole audit without GPU compute is a deliberate constraint: an evaluation instrument that requires a cluster is one most teams will never run against their own system.

Refusing to accept a model on its own metrics

Every candidate model was scored by AUC against a popularity baseline before it could be released. A recommender that cannot beat "show the bestsellers" is an expensive way to do nothing. That check was an early, informal version of what the research above now does deliberately, and it ran in production years before I had a name for it.

The setting made that check necessary. The signal is implicit — nobody rates anything, they just buy — the matrix is overwhelmingly sparse, and a degraded model fails silently, returning ten confident items that are wrong. When every API call is charged, plausible-but-wrong stops being a modelling inconvenience and becomes a billing dispute. We modelled implicit feedback with ALS, weighting confidence so a repeat purchase outweighs a single glance, and masked already-purchased items from every ranking.

Serving is a search problem: embeddings precomputed during training, queried through an approximate nearest-neighbour index in 64 dimensions. Training is dispatched over Redis to isolated Celery workers with hard time ceilings and in-task memory guards, every run versioned in MLflow, exactly one run ever marked servable, and quota checked before inference rather than after. Two real problems solved: next-restaurant prediction across 18,948 delivery users, and a WooCommerce catalogue turned into a live endpoint on a five-minute sync.

Research notes

Personalization with a verification boundary

Margin adapts to one learner: two-stage mastery, spaced review on a 3 → 7 → 14 → 30-day ladder, and error patterns from your last three graded essays pulling matching lessons forward. That makes it a personalization system, and personalization built on a language model inherits the model's failure modes. A hallucinated band score sends a candidate into a real exam prepared against a false signal.

So it is also the working test of the thesis: a probabilistic model does the hard reasoning, and deterministic code decides what the learner is allowed to see. Every response crosses a validation boundary before it renders. Eighteen normalizers treat provider output as hostile input. Scores are clamped to each exam's legal lattice, band aggregates are recomputed in code as the weighted mean of their components so a headline can never contradict the numbers beneath it, and the client owns every state machine rather than trusting the model's account of where it is. Each guard exists because a real provider broke that rule. The 254 teaching entries are hand-authored rather than generated, so they cannot hallucinate or vary between runs.

The runtime carries a reasoning-budget escalation ladder: a model that burns its allowance thinking and returns nothing is retried with more headroom instead of billing for an answer nobody received. Truncation is caught through finish_reason, and every call reserves credits under a row lock, then finalizes or refunds.

Negative result, kept

I raised an output ceiling I believed was truncating explanations, then measured output at roughly 1,128 tokens against the original 1,250 limit. The hypothesis failed, and the finding stayed in the record.

An agent is only as trustworthy as its refusal path

A bank chatbot that invents an account rule doesn't produce a bad answer — it produces a compliance incident. The hard engineering here was never the answer path. It was everything the model is not allowed to do.

Retrieval is the contract: agents answer only from tenant-approved content indexed at ingestion, and anything generated outside that corpus is a failure mode rather than a feature. Every response carries a confidence score, and below the configured threshold the agent stops and routes the customer to a hotline, branch or human agent instead of producing something plausible. A refusal costs a click; a confident wrong number costs a customer.

In multi-tenant RAG, retrieval leakage is a breach rather than a bug, so isolation is enforced at the DynamoDB partition key rather than in the prompt. CloudWatch traces every agent decision, and usage meters in tokens against a per-tenant wallet so cost stays attributable down to the agent and channel. Sentiment scoring escalates frustrated conversations to human agents, who receive suggested replies rather than autonomous ones — the model drafts, a person sends. Every out-of-scope query is logged against the knowledge base, so refusals become the backlog of what the agent still cannot answer.

Open gap

That feedback loop is human-reviewed today. Nothing retrains itself yet. Next to close.

Choosing not to automate the step that would fail invisibly

Drawings arrive as PDFs, rasterized at 300 DPI, with all measurement in image space. The geometry is not the hard part. A pixel means nothing until you know what it is worth. Each drawing carries scale factors validated as positive before any conversion runs, because getting that wrong prices thousands of materials against a fiction.

Automatic detection of walls and openings was the obvious next step, and we did not take it. A misdetected edge produces a confident wrong number nobody can see; a badly traced edge is visibly wrong. The contractor traces, the system measures, and the pipeline reports its own duration, point count and success rate across a rolling window.

Known gap

When calibration is missing, the converter falls back to raw pixel units instead of refusing. Fail-open, and next to fix.
